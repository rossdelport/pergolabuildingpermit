Pergola vs patio: the real difference

Short answer

It's the roof. A pergola is open or has a permeable covering like shade cloth. A patio has a solid, waterproof roof. That changes the permit rules: a pergola with a house is exempt up to 20 m², but a freestanding patio only up to 10 m² (both 2.4 m high or less).

Why it matters

Calling something a pergola doesn't make it one. What counts is whether the top lets water through. Add a solid roof to a pergola and, legally, it's now a patio. That brings the smaller 10 m² exemption limit and, if it's attached, the need for a building permit. See putting a roof on a pergola.

Shade sails follow the same idea. The Town of Victoria Park, for example, treats an impermeable (vinyl) shade sail as a verandah and a permeable one as a pergola.

Planning rules

The R-Codes' definition of a "building" leaves pergolas out, but includes patios. Patios have to meet setbacks; at Wanneroo, for example, 1 m from the side boundary to the post for a patio up to 14 m long. Check your council's rules for pergolas too, especially near boundaries or in the front yard.

Louvre roofs

Opening louvre roofs sit between the two. They're watertight when closed, so ask your council how it classifies yours before you build.

Questions

Common questions

What is the difference between a pergola and a patio in WA?

The roof. A pergola has no roof, or only an open-weave permeable covering like shade cloth. A patio has a water-impermeable roof, such as metal or polycarbonate.

Does a pergola need a permit in WA?

A pergola built with a house doesn't need a building permit if it's 20 m² or less and 2.4 m or lower. Bigger or taller ones do.

Does a patio need a permit in WA?

A freestanding patio doesn't if it's 10 m² or less and 2.4 m or lower. Councils such as Wanneroo require a building permit for every attached patio.

If I add a roof to my pergola, is it a patio?

Yes. Once the covering is water-impermeable, it meets the definition of a patio and the patio rules apply.
